Universities Aren't Altruistic Monoliths – They're More Like a Collection of Small Businesses…

Jul 20, 2026 · Category: Grad School, Career, Personal Development, Entrepreneurship

In May 2024, as I was forced to switch PhD topics for the third time in my nearly three years in the PhD at that point, I reached out to nearly everyone I knew asking advice for how I could potentially still salvage my PhD (or if it was still worth saving) at that point.

One of the conversations I had with a professor who I greatly admire changed the way I viewed universities completely.

They mentioned that the best way to think of universities is roughly as follows.

Universities are like a collection of small businesses. Some of those businesses are thriving and are ones that you want to align yourself with, and some aren't.

Essentially, the professor mentioned that universities are NOT a monolith — instead, they're more like a mini town or city that's a collection of a whole bunch of different small businesses.

Like in a real city, some of those businesses are thriving, and some are failing.

Like in the real world, you want to align yourself with the businesses which are either successful, profitable, or growing, and avoid businesses which aren't doing so well.

The University of Southern California campus — essentially a collection of small businesses under one roof.

Let's dive into a few quick examples.

A Personal Example

When I was recently a GNC intern at Blue Origin this spring, there was another GNC intern who was finishing his degree in Aerospace at Stanford.

He went to Stanford for his Bachelors and Masters — I went to the University of Southern California for my Master's in Astronautical Engineering (and half of my PhD).

When we hopped on a call, something we found out we had in common is that despite being at very prestigious, top universities at top aero/astro programs in California, we had both previously worked in very underfunded plasma physics labs at our respective institutions.

Similar to me and my situation with my contamination research, he realized that plasma physics wasn't very lucrative, so for his last couple of years or so at Stanford, he transferred to one of the more prestigious space autonomy and GNC labs at Stanford.

In his own words, "the funding (and support he got) was night and day" between the plasma physics and GNC labs that he worked in at Stanford.

This is something I also similarly observed when transitioning from my own advisor's plasma physics lab at USC to my GNC lab at Blue Origin — the amount of money that was being invested into my specific project was also "night and day."

As a corollary, switching from contamination / plasma physics to GNC not only led to a more well-funded environment for both of us, but much better job opportunities and prospects as well.

The takeaway is that some PhD labs (and/or other parts of the university, as well as industry companies as well) are swimming in money, whereas many others struggle badly despite seemingly being the same from the outside.

Universities are NOT a monolith. There are some amazing and thriving parts of the university that you absolutely want to align yourself with, and there are some deep dark crevices and corners that you can (and don't want to) get trapped in.

Two people can have drastically different experiences despite going to the same school.

A Few More Examples

A more polarizing but illustrative example of a university simply being a collection of corporations or small businesses is the Football and Basketball Teams.

Both of those sports teams are essentially their own independent corporations under the larger umbrella of the university, and are massive revenue and profit drivers, just like an NFL or NBA team would be within their own city.

At USC and many major universities, the health system is by far the biggest revenue driver for the university (resulting in 48% of all revenue, whereas tuition for example only amounts to 24% of USC's revenues for 2025).

In this case, this component of USC is much more like a "big business" and ethics aside, it provides a ton of profits, just like a real hospital system would in real life. Furthermore, it supports and provides plentiful funding to a lot of Doctoral and Master's students and their respective research labs.

In a strong contrast to my PhD lab, I had friends on the health sciences campus who were PhD students who worked in larger teams, didn't work completely on their own, had catered lunches (and sometimes even dinners) everyday, had plenty of money and funding for their PhD labs, and they also graduated with their PhDs in 3–3.5 years whereas I (and many of the people in my department) were looking at 5–7 year PhD (or sometimes worse).

On the surface, we had the same title — "University of Southern California PhD Student" — but our experiences couldn't have been any different since they were in a very financially healthy "business" within the university whereas I wasn't as much.

The same goes for non health sciences labs as well — there are some labs on campus which are incredibly healthy from a financial perspective (plentiful funding, grants, support, etc.).

Outside of health sciences, some that I can think of are AI, Machine Learning, Autonomy, and Robotics type labs that exist on campus.

Many of the Undergraduate, Masters, and PhD students who work in those types of labs will build skills and career capital which will lead to highly lucrative careers when they graduate.

Tangible Takeaways

First, as you saw in the Stanford and USC examples, prestige does not fully matter.

In a vacuum, it helps if you take advantage of it – I've gained tremendous benefits from going to USC and having that brand on my resume – but it can't really overcome aligning yourself with the wrong people (or "businesses") within the university.

To expand on that, whether you're at a top university or not, you need to align yourself with professors, labs, or other extracurriculars which have a lot of steam behind them.

This is a lot like joining a real-world business which has a lot of momentum behind it or a startup which has a ton of funding and upcoming growth upside.

If you join a more stagnant or "declining" part of the university, you might waste precious time, end up wasting several years of your early career, and set yourself back years compared to where you could've been.

This would've been me along with the Stanford guy had we stayed in our respective plasma physics labs long-term.

Professors are sort of like business owners in a way, and there's some that are the equivalent of brilliant "founders" who you absolutely want to work with, and some who are just skating by or even failing. Make sure you work with the former.

Remember — Universities are simply a collection of small businesses. Make sure you align yourself with the highest growth "businesses" and best "founders" with a lot of steam behind them and you'll set yourself up for success.

Appendix: Relative Career Capital

One caveat I'll mention is that your outcomes aren't only influenced by the "business" within the university that you join, but also by your own standing and leverage within it.

For example, in 2023, star quarterback and football player Caleb Williams and I both had the title of "USC Student" but had drastically different opportunities since he was very, very good at something which paid well.

As a result, he received much better perks than I did — free apartments, meals, millions in NIL deals, and so on; and he eventually became the number 1 pick in the NFL draft.

On the other hand, there might've been someone on the football team (e.g. the 53rd man on the roster) who didn't have the same level of talent, and despite being in the same part of the university, received a lot less opportunities beyond their normal full ride scholarship and other perks (e.g. not millions in NIL deals, or free stuff).

And even beyond that, there might be an athlete who's maybe just as talented and hard-working, but doesn't even get the scholarship since they're in a less lucrative sport within the university which doesn't generate any revenue and therefore doesn't hand out full ride scholarships to all of its players. They might even be struggling to pay for tuition, who knows.

So while the opportunity vehicle you pick within the university can have a huge impact on your outcomes, your relative skillset and leverage coming into the university in the first place can have a huge impact too.

Being the 53rd or 25th man on the USC Football team will probably land you better opportunities than being the top person on, say, the water polo or rowing teams – the opportunity vehicle you pick absolutely matters.

But your own value and personal standing within that opportunity vehicle matters just as much – it's the combination of the two that really moves the needle.

Ideally, you'll both pick the right vehicle, and get good – this is where you'll maximize your opportunity at university.

There's a saying I learned from an older Astronautical Engineering PhD Student who mentioned that "if you bring in money, you will be favored," and that definitely rings true in careers and life!
